Lab Report Reflection Questions & Solutions

Key biomechanical mechanisms for students to answer in their BME 4903 lab report. Click each question to reveal the biomechanical rationale.

1 Impact Attenuation & Impulse-Momentum Principles

Question: Why does landing with forward trunk lean reduce peak vGRF and loading rate compared to upright landing?

Biomechanical Rationale: By Newton's Second Law and the impulse-momentum theorem, $J = \Delta p = \int_{t_{IC}}^{t_{KM}} F\, dt = m \Delta v$. Because downward velocity upon contact is determined by jump height, the required total change in momentum $\Delta p$ is nearly identical between trials. Forward trunk lean facilitates much greater knee flexion (78.9° vs 54.0°) and hip flexion, lengthening the deceleration window by over 2.5× (0.433 s vs 0.167 s). Spreading the momentum change over a longer duration substantially reduces the peak force (4.10 BW vs 4.45 BW) and slashes the impact loading rate by 66% (18.6 BW/s vs 55.0 BW/s).

2 Biarticular Rectus Femoris Force-Length Mechanics

Question: Why does the rectus femoris experience ~70% higher peak force in the upright posture (1,270 N vs 749 N)?

Biomechanical Rationale: The rectus femoris is the only biarticular muscle of the quadriceps, crossing both the anterior hip (as a flexor) and anterior knee (as an extensor). In an upright landing (extended hip), the muscle fibers of the rectus femoris are stretched across the hip joint. Under the sliding filament force-length relationship, this placed the muscle fibers in a more optimal, stretched region that generates high passive and active tension. Conversely, forward trunk lean flexes the hip, passively shortening the rectus femoris at its proximal attachment, thereby reducing its force contribution.

3 Vasti Muscle Sharing & Vastus Lateralis Dominance

Question: Which individual vastus muscle generates the largest force, and why?

Biomechanical Rationale: The vastus lateralis (vaslat) generates the largest absolute force (4,831 N upright, 3,526 N leaning). This is because the vastus lateralis possesses the largest physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA) among all four quadriceps heads in human musculoskeletal models. It provides the primary eccentric torque required to prevent knee collapse during impact absorption.

4 ACL Injury Prevention & Tibial Shear Mechanics

Question: Which landing posture creates greater risk for Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) tears, and why?

Biomechanical Rationale: The upright landing posture poses a substantially greater risk for non-contact ACL injuries. When the knee is flexed at a shallow angle (< 60°), the patellar tendon inserts onto the tibial tuberosity at an acute angle that directs a large component of quadriceps force anteriorly. With upright peak quadriceps forces exceeding 7,870 N, this generates extreme anterior tibial shear force, directly straining the ACL. Coaching athletes to land with moderate forward trunk flexion and deep knee flexion (> 70°) reduces patellar tendon anterior shear and protects the ligament.

5 Hip vs. Knee Energy Dissipation (The Posterior Chain)

Question: In forward-lean landing, knee extensor demand is reduced. What muscle groups absorb the remaining energy?

Biomechanical Rationale: Forward trunk flexion moves the body Center of Mass forward, shifting the ground reaction force vector closer to the knee joint axis (reducing the external knee flexion moment arm) and farther from the hip joint axis (increasing the external hip flexion moment arm). Consequently, mechanical energy dissipation is transferred from the anterior knee extensors to the powerful posterior chain hip extensors—specifically the gluteus maximus and hamstrings—which safely absorb impact energy without loading the patellar tendon or ACL.
